The Price of Water: How Flood Risk Is Rewriting Insurance, Lending, and Home Values

Flood is no longer a footnote in a policy or a line on a disclosure form. It is a price — and prices, once they move, change behavior.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · October 29, 2026 · 10 min read

The closing was supposed to take twenty minutes. In a title office off US 41 in Fort Myers, a young couple watched the numbers on the settlement statement shift a second time. The house was not on the beach. It sat three streets back from a canal, in a subdivision built in the 1990s, on ground that had never flooded in the sellers' memory. Then the lender's flood determination arrived, and the insurance quote that followed it was four times the figure in their first budget. The house had not changed. The water had not arrived. The price of living near it had.

That scene is the story of American housing in the 2020s. Flood is no longer a footnote in a policy or a line on a disclosure form. It is a price. And prices, once they move, change behavior in ways that speeches about climate never do.

The scale explains why. The Federal Emergency Management Agency has long mapped about 8.7 million properties inside its Special Flood Hazard Areas, the zones where federally backed mortgages require flood insurance. First Street Foundation, a nonprofit research group founded by Matthew Eby, estimated in 2020 that roughly 14.6 million properties actually face substantial flood risk — nearly 70 percent more than the federal maps showed. The gap was not a rounding error. It was a different picture of the country, built from elevation, rainfall, river gauges, and storm surge rather than from paper maps decades old in many counties.

Hurricanes supplied the rest of the education. Hurricane Harvey in 2017 dropped 51 inches of rain near Cedar Bayou, Texas, and caused about $125 billion in damage, according to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. Much of that loss was uninsured. Hurricane Ian in 2022 caused about $112 billion in damage in Florida and the Carolinas, NOAA reported, and became one of the costliest storms in American history. Hurricane Helene in 2024, which flooded mountain towns in western North Carolina far from any coast, caused roughly $78 billion in damage by NOAA's accounting and killed more than 200 people. Each storm taught a new set of homeowners that flood risk does not respect the coastline, the flood zone boundary, or the phrase "it never floods here."

The National Flood Insurance Program, the federal backstop that still covers most American flood policies, felt the strain first. The program carried more than $20 billion in debt to the U.S. Treasury in the years after Harvey, Irma, and Maria, even after Congress canceled $16 billion of earlier borrowing in 2017. With roughly 4.7 million policies in force, it remained the largest single flood insurer in the country — and the clearest proof that the old pricing method, which grouped vast numbers of homes into broad zones and subsidized many of them, could not hold. In October 2021, FEMA replaced it with Risk Rating 2.0, a system that prices each property on its own characteristics: distance to water, elevation relative to flood levels, construction type, and replacement cost.

Private capital read the same signals and moved faster. Swiss Re, the world's second-largest reinsurer, reported global insured losses from natural catastrophes above $100 billion in 2023, with storms and floods contributing a growing share. Munich Re put overall global natural-catastrophe losses in 2024 at about $140 billion. CoreLogic, the property data firm, has built flood models that lenders and insurers now use to score individual addresses. Neptune Flood, a private flood insurer founded by Jim Albert, grew by selling policies priced address by address in states where the federal program's rates had lagged the risk for years.

For homeowners, the result is uneven and often painful. Carolyn Kousky, a researcher who has studied flood insurance at the University of Pennsylvania's Wharton Risk Center and later at the Environmental Defense Fund, has documented a persistent problem: the households least able to pay higher premiums are often the ones living in the riskiest places, including older, lower-income neighborhoods built on low ground. A premium rising from $600 to $3,000 a year does not merely annoy a family. It can break a mortgage qualification, force a sale, or leave a house uninsured.

Lenders are now part of the pricing chain whether they want to be or not. A mortgage backed by Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac requires flood insurance when a property sits in a mapped hazard area, and the cost of that insurance feeds directly into the borrower's debt-to-income calculation. When premiums jump, loans fail. When appraisers begin to reflect flood exposure in comparable sales, values follow. Research on repeat-sales data after major floods has repeatedly found price discounts for flooded and high-risk homes, discounts that persist for years rather than fading with the next dry season. The market, in other words, is learning to discount water the way it once learned to discount a bad school district or a noisy highway — only the discount here can be an order of magnitude larger.

Skeptics push back, and their objections deserve a fair hearing. Some argue that private flood models, built on proprietary data and assumptions, overstate risk in ways homeowners cannot check or appeal. Others warn that mandatory disclosure of a risk score can brand a neighborhood and depress values before any adaptation — elevation, drainage, levees — has a chance to work. Still others note that the federal maps, for all their flaws, at least offered a single public standard, while a patchwork of private scores can leave two neighbors with similar houses and wildly different bills. These are real objections. A pricing system that cannot be explained will not be trusted, and a system that punishes people for risks they did not create and cannot afford to fix will face political reversal.

The counterargument, though, does not restore the old world. Subsidized, zone-based pricing hid risk; it did not remove it. When Harvey flooded homes outside the mapped hazard area, the bill arrived anyway — paid by taxpayers, charities, and uninsured families rather than by premiums. Granular pricing at least puts the cost where the risk sits, and gives owners, builders, and local governments a reason to elevate, to drain, to set back, and to stop building slab-on-grade homes in known floodways. The question is not whether flood will be priced. It is whether the pricing will be accurate, transparent, and paired with help for the people it hits hardest.

That is where the next decade will be decided. NOAA projects that sea level along the United States coastline will rise another 10 to 12 inches by 2050 relative to 2020, a shift that turns today's rare flood into tomorrow's routine one in low-lying places. The firms that win in this market will not be the ones that merely publish a scarier map. They will be the ones that can explain a number to a homeowner, defend it to a regulator, and connect it to a concrete action — raise the house two feet, move the mechanicals, buy the right limit — that lowers the next quote. Price is information. In flood, for the first time, the information is finally catching up with the water.
