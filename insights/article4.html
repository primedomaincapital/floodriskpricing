The Private Market Is Eating the NFIP's Lunch — Slowly

Private flood insurers now write more than a quarter of the nation's flood premiums. The federal program still sets the price of the risk — but it no longer sets the price of the policy.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 9, 2026 · 9 min read

On October 20, 2026, FEMA did something the National Flood Insurance Program had never done: it let a homeowner buy an NFIP policy online, directly, without an agent in the middle. The announcement was framed as convenience. Read it again and it is a defensive move. The NFIP is losing the customers it can least afford to lose — the newer, elevated, lower-risk homes whose premiums used to subsidize everyone else — and it is losing them to private insurers who price each house on its own merits and keep the good risks for themselves.

The numbers behind the shift are stark. The national average NFIP premium sits near $926 a year, against a private-market average of about $1,140 a year, according to S&P Global figures compiled in 2026 guides. That average hides the selection at work: private cover is cheaper about 60 percent of the time for newer or elevated homes, often by 20 to 40 percent, and Neptune Flood, the largest private flood carrier, averages $985 a year. Private flood insurance accounted for about 27 percent of flood premiums in 2024, up from 13 percent in 2016. In a decade, the private share doubled. The NFIP's own data explains why the trend points one way: roughly 77 percent of policyholders saw premiums rise under Risk Rating 2.0, while 23 percent saw them fall — and the ones who saw them fall were disproportionately inland properties whose old zone-based rates had overestimated their risk. The private market is, in effect, harvesting the 23 percent.

Risk Rating 2.0, fully in force since April 2023, is what made the harvest possible. FEMA replaced zone-based pricing with property-specific pricing — flood frequency, flood type, distance to water, elevation, and rebuilding cost — which means two homes on the same street can now pay wildly different premiums. The NFIP's national average of about $976 a year from 2026 data breaks apart on inspection: high-risk zones average $1,114, other zones $745, and Florida's average runs $2,837 against a median of $1,289, a spread that says everything about how concentrated coastal risk has become. Annual increases are capped at 18 percent for primary residences and 25 percent for second homes, so a coastal property whose full risk-rated price is $3,000 will climb there from $1,200 within a few years. The cap slows the repricing. It does not stop it.

The private carriers' advantage is not just price. It is speed and limits. An NFIP policy carries a 30-day waiting period; private policies typically bind in 10 to 14 days. NFIP building coverage caps at $250,000; private carriers write above it. And private underwriting is free to decline the risks it does not want, while the NFIP, by design, must take them. That asymmetry is the whole story: the federal program is the insurer of last resort for the properties the private market will not touch, and the private market is the insurer of first resort for the properties it will. A program built on cross-subsidy cannot survive the loss of the subsidizers.

Here is the counterargument, and it deserves more respect than the private market's marketing allows. The NFIP exists precisely because the private market once refused flood risk altogether — the program was created in 1968 after private insurers walked away from flood cover entirely. Private flood insurance today is concentrated, cyclical, and untested by a truly catastrophic season: a carrier that writes 27 percent of premiums can also withdraw 27 percent of premiums, and it can do so county by county, after a single bad hurricane. The NFIP cannot withdraw. It also cannot go bankrupt in the ordinary sense; it borrows from the Treasury. A homeowner who saves 30 percent with a private carrier is buying a cheaper policy from a company that is allowed to leave. A homeowner who stays with the NFIP is buying an expensive policy from a program that is not.

The middle of the market is where the pricing gets interesting. FEMA's own figures note that about one in four flood claims comes from properties outside the high-risk zones, and more than 40 percent of claims arise outside those zones by some counts. The low-to-moderate-risk home — Zone X, $400 to $700 a year through the NFIP — is exactly the property a private carrier wants, and exactly the property whose premium subsidized the coastal book. Communities in FEMA's Community Rating System can earn discounts of up to 45 percent for NFIP policyholders, a lever no private carrier offers, and it rewards the one thing both markets agree on: mitigation. Elevation, distance, and construction are the only variables that lower the price in either system.

The October online-purchase option is FEMA's answer to the selection problem, and it is a revealing one. Making the NFIP easier to buy does not make it cheaper, and it does not change the risk it must carry. What it does is defend the program's distribution at the moment its best risks are walking out the door. The program's defenders will call that modernization. Its critics will call it a subsidy for the website of a program whose pricing has already been outflanked.

The implication for a homeowner is a comparison that has to be run property by property, not program by program. Get the NFIP quote and the private quote for the same house, in the same week, and read the waiting period, the building cap, and the carrier's right to non-renew as carefully as the premium. The national averages — $926 against $1,140 — will tell you nothing about your house, because Risk Rating 2.0 made sure of that. The market has split into two insurers with opposite business models: one that must take every risk, and one that may take only the good ones. The price of flood cover now depends on which of those two businesses wants your address.
