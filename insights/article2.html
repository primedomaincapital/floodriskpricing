The Map Is No Longer the Territory: How Flood Models Replaced the Flood Zone

For half a century, a line on a federal map governed American flood finance. The models have replaced it — property by property.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · October 29, 2026 · 9 min read

Roy Wright used to carry a simple message to town halls. When he ran the National Flood Insurance Program's mitigation work at FEMA, he would tell homeowners that a line on a federal map was not a promise. Water did not read maps. A house outside the line could flood. A house inside it might never take a drop. People nodded. Then they went home and priced their houses as if the line were the truth anyway.

For half a century, that line governed American flood finance. FEMA's Flood Insurance Rate Maps divided the country into zones, and a property's zone, plus its elevation certificate, largely set its premium. The system had virtues: it was public, it was uniform, and any agent could explain it in a minute. It also had a flaw that grew harder to ignore with every storm. The maps were slow, coarse, and often old. In many counties, the map in force dated to the 1980s or 1990s, drawn before subdivisions filled the fields behind the levee and before rainfall records absorbed three decades of heavier downpours. A zone could cover thousands of homes with different elevations, different construction, and different distances to the nearest river — and charge many of them nearly the same price.

Risk Rating 2.0 ended that bargain. Rolled out for new policies in October 2021 and for renewals the following April, FEMA's new method prices flood the way auto insurers price drivers: property by property. Distance to a river or coast counts. So does elevation relative to the expected flood level, the cost to rebuild the structure, and the type of flooding a location faces — riverine overflow, storm surge, intense rainfall, or coastal erosion. David Maurstad, then the senior executive for the National Flood Insurance Program, presented the change as a correction of a long-standing inequity: under the old zones, lower-value homes often subsidized higher-value ones, because premiums reflected zone and subsidized rates more than they reflected the actual exposure of each building.

FEMA's own estimates showed how uneven the correction would be. The agency said about 23 percent of policyholders would see their premiums fall under the new method, while about two-thirds would see increases of $10 a month or less. A smaller group — roughly 11 percent combined — would face larger monthly increases, in some cases far larger. In practice, the averages hid sharp local stories. A slab-on-grade ranch a quarter-mile from a bayou could see a bill triple. A raised home two streets over, behind the same levee, could see its bill drop. Neighbors compared letters at kitchen tables and reached the same conclusion: the map they had trusted was never really measuring their houses at all.

Private modelers had been making the same argument for years, with better tools. First Street Foundation, led by founder Matthew Eby, built a national model that combined elevation data, hydrology, and climate projections to score individual parcels. Its 2020 finding — about 14.6 million properties at substantial risk, against roughly 8.7 million inside FEMA's high-risk zones — became a reference point for journalists, lenders, and investors. CoreLogic, whose property records underpin much of American mortgage and insurance underwriting, added flood scores that lenders could run across entire portfolios in an afternoon. Milliman, the actuarial firm, advised carriers on how to translate those scores into rates that state regulators would accept. The federal program was no longer the only map in town, and in many markets it was no longer the most detailed one.

The data behind the models keeps getting harder to dismiss. NOAA recorded 28 separate billion-dollar weather and climate disasters in the United States in 2023, with total costs of about $92.9 billion, and 27 such events in 2024 at roughly $182.7 billion — figures that include hurricanes, floods, and severe storms together, and that show how routine the extraordinary has become. In 2016, floods in Baton Rouge damaged more than 100,000 homes, and the majority of affected properties sat outside the mapped high-risk zone. In 2019, the Mississippi River stayed above flood stage at Baton Rouge for months, a slow-motion event that maps built for a single storm surge never captured well. Rainfall, not just surge, now drives a large share of flood loss, and rainfall is precisely what the old zone system measured worst.

None of this means the new models are beyond challenge. Critics raise three serious objections, and each has force. First, private models are often black boxes: a homeowner told that her score is 7.3 out of 10 cannot easily audit the elevation input, the rainfall assumption, or the weighting that produced it. Second, granular pricing can punish places that are already struggling. A low-income neighborhood built on low ground in the 1950s did not choose its elevation, and a premium its residents cannot pay may simply push them out of insurance altogether — the worst outcome for both the family and the taxpayer who ultimately funds disaster aid. Third, models disagree. Run the same address through three vendors and you can get three different answers, which leaves regulators, lenders, and homeowners asking which number is the real one. Congress has heard all three objections, and bills to cap Risk Rating 2.0 increases or restore older pricing have been introduced in every session since the rollout.

Those objections point to a design problem, not a reason to return to zone pricing. A credible flood price needs to be explainable in plain language: this is your elevation, this is your distance to water, this is what happens if you elevate two feet or move your furnace out of the basement. It needs to be contestable, with a clear path to correct a bad elevation reading or a misclassified building. And it needs to be paired with mitigation funding — FEMA's own Flood Mitigation Assistance grants, state elevation programs, and the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act's $3.5 billion for flood mitigation — so that a higher price is a signal to act rather than simply a bill to dread. Roy Wright, after leaving FEMA to lead the Insurance Institute for Business and Home Safety, has made a version of this argument for years: the cheapest flood loss is the one a stronger building never suffers.

The direction of travel is now clear even to the skeptics. Lenders run portfolio flood scores before they buy loans. Reinsurers price treaties on modeled loss, not on zone counts. Homebuyers in Florida, Texas, and the Carolinas increasingly ask for a flood score the way an earlier generation asked for a roof age or a school rating. The old question — are you in or out of the zone? — is giving way to a harder, better one: how high is this house, how close is the water, and what would it cost when the water comes? Maps will still matter. They will simply no longer be allowed to pretend they are the whole answer.
